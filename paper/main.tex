\begin{abstract}
Putting predictive-maintenance (PdM) models on a factory floor raises questions that accuracy scores leave open: where inference runs, how far a prediction can be trusted, and when a person decides. We study them with HERA-PdM. Cross-validated conformalized quantile regression (CQR-CV+) turns remaining-useful-life (RUL) predictions into intervals, the edge escalates only when its interval does not settle the maintenance action, and costly actions go to a human as SHAP-backed recommendations. On 17 FEMTO and 15 XJTU-SY run-to-failure bearings, CQR-CV+ raised coverage to \fePicpEdge--\fePicpCloud{} and \xjPicpCloud--\xjPicpEdge{} (nominal 0.90), and calibrated decisions cut critical snapshots left without action from \feUnsafePoint\% to \feUnsafeHera\% (FEMTO) and \xjUnsafePoint\% to \xjUnsafeHera\% (XJTU-SY), at the cost of more false maintenance. Escalation helped only under gradual degradation: on XJTU-SY, escalating \xjEscHera\% of snapshots matched always-cloud safety with less false maintenance (\xjFmHera\% vs.\ \xjFmCloud\%), though not for every bearing. Our gate did no better than a plain width gate, and per-bearing alarms came too early because a dozen calibration units are too few for risk control. On \flN{} Scania trucks, the same risk control met its lateness target (\flHeBigLate\% vs.\ \flFixClLate\% for a fixed threshold) and HERA escalated \flHeBigEsc\% of readouts at always-cloud safety, but false alarms stayed high.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Predictive maintenance (PdM) estimates the remaining useful life (RUL) of a machine from condition data so that repairs can be planned before failure~\cite{lei2018machinery}. Running it on a real factory floor raises three questions that accuracy alone does not answer. \emph{Where} should inference run? Vibration is sensed at the machine, and edge--cloud architectures keep routine inference local~\cite{ren2021cloudedge}. \emph{How far} can a prediction be trusted? That calls for calibrated uncertainty, not point estimates~\cite{romano2019cqr}. And \emph{who} acts on it? Industry~5.0 keeps people in charge of consequential decisions~\cite{xu2021industry5}.

Hierarchical PdM systems combining edge devices, Digital Twins (DTs) and agents already exist~\cite{saleh2026hama,yao2026ecdistill}. They escalate from edge to cloud on anomaly thresholds~\cite{saleh2026hama} or uncalibrated confidence~\cite{yao2026ecdistill}. Calibrated deferral with guarantees exists for classification cascades~\cite{huang2025cab}, but not for RUL-based maintenance. We therefore ask \emph{when} conformally calibrated RUL intervals computed on the edge are a useful escalation signal, and what they cost in safety, false maintenance, alarm timing, communication and latency.

\textit{Key findings.} Across two bearing datasets with very different degradation and a truck fleet, (i)~calibration removes most unsafe point-threshold decisions; (ii)~escalation pays off only when degradation shows early; (iii)~interval width matters more than the gating rule; (iv)~per-snapshot guarantees do not give timely per-bearing alarms; and (v)~unit-level lateness guarantees need fleets, not a dozen bearings.

Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item HERA-PdM, a reproducible four-layer framework whose \emph{decision-sufficiency} gate escalates only when the edge RUL interval straddles a maintenance-decision boundary, which bounds locally resolved unsafe decisions by the edge miscoverage level.
\item A bearing-grouped, two-dataset benchmark of ten tuned quantile models with and without DT context, showing that CQR-CV+ restores nominal coverage and that larger fog/cloud models help only under gradual degradation.
\item Snapshot-, unit- and fleet-level evidence with bootstrap intervals, and a feasibility bound for unit-level risk control (Proposition~1) checked on \flN{} trucks, distilled into deployment lessons.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work and Research Gap}\label{sec:related}
\textit{RUL prediction.} Deep sequence models such as temporal convolutional networks~\cite{bai2018tcn} are now standard RUL estimators. Reviews~\cite{lei2018machinery} treat health-stage division, that is, detecting when degradation first becomes observable, as a prerequisite for prognosis. Our datasets~\cite{nectoux2012pronostia,wang2020xjtu} differ sharply here.
\textit{Hierarchical PdM.} Ren et~al.~\cite{ren2021cloudedge} run a lightweight TCN on the edge and refine its estimate in the cloud. HAMA~\cite{saleh2026hama} escalates from an edge z-score pre-filter to fog ensembles with SHAP and generated operator text. EC-Distill-ZeroDiag~\cite{yao2026ecdistill} hands uncertain fault-diagnosis cases from an edge agent to a cloud model.
\textit{Calibrated uncertainty.} CQR~\cite{romano2019cqr} and CV+~\cite{barber2021jackknife} give coverage without distributional assumptions. Conformal RUL intervals have been built for bearings~\cite{wang2026conformal}, but not tied to a decision or routing layer. Conformal risk control~\cite{angelopoulos2024crc} bounds expected monotone losses, and conformal alignment certifies edge--cloud cascades for classification~\cite{huang2025cab}.

\textit{Gap.} We coded 42 papers (Table~\ref{tab:related}). None uses conformal RUL intervals computed on the edge as the escalation rule of a hierarchical PdM system, links escalation to maintenance-decision boundaries, and compares it with the gates of existing systems on public run-to-failure data from different degradation regimes. The architecture itself is not new; our contribution lies in the method and its evaluation.

\section{HERA-PdM Methodology}
\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/fig1_architecture.pdf}
\caption{HERA-PdM. The edge agent computes a calibrated RUL interval $I^e_t$; the gate escalates only if $I^e_t$ is not decision-sufficient; the fog/cloud agent uses the DT context $c_t$; schedule/urgent actions need human approval.}
\label{fig:arch}
\end{figure}

Fig.~\ref{fig:arch} shows the four layers. The ``agents'' are software components with fixed responsibilities and messages; no language model is involved.

\textit{Edge Monitoring Agent.} Each vibration snapshot is reduced to 28 features $x_t\in\mathbb{R}^{28}$ (per axis: RMS, kurtosis, skewness, peak-to-peak, crest, impulse and shape factors, spectral centroid and six log band energies). They are z-scored against the asset's first snapshots and compressed by $\mathrm{sign}(z)\log(1{+}|z|)$, giving the health index $h_t=\log(1+\frac1d\sum_j|z_{t,j}|)$. The anomaly score $a_t$, the smoothed $h_t$ standardized by the asset's raw baseline, alarms above $\tau_a$, the 99th percentile of healthy training snapshots. A tiny model maps a short window to three RUL quantiles.

\textit{Digital Twin State Agent.} The twin is a synchronized state record $DT_t=\{$condition, sensor state, $h_t$, context $c_t$, $\hat R_t$, $I_t$, $u_t$, anomaly state$\}$, updated by a 12-byte sync $(t,h_t,a_t)$ after each snapshot. The context $c_t\in\mathbb{R}^6$ holds fast and slow averages of $h_t$, their difference, its running maximum, its slope and the cumulative excess over baseline. Absolute operating time is left out because lifetimes differ by an order of magnitude.

\textit{RUL Prognostics Agent.} A sequence model $g_\phi$ takes a longer window $X_t$ together with $c_t$ and outputs non-crossing quantiles $(\hat q^{\,\alpha/2}_t,\hat q^{\,0.5}_t,\hat q^{\,1-\alpha/2}_t)$, trained with the pinball loss on the capped target $y_t=\min(\mathrm{RUL}_t,R_{\max})$.

\textit{Calibration (CQR-CV+).} We split the non-test assets into $K{=}4$ groups balanced by condition and train an inner model $g^{(-k)}$ without group $k$. This yields an out-of-fold score $s_i=\max(\hat q^{\,\alpha/2}_i-y_i,\,y_i-\hat q^{\,1-\alpha/2}_i)$ for every non-test snapshot $i$ in group $k(i)$. Following CV+~\cite{barber2021jackknife} with CQR scores~\cite{romano2019cqr},
\begin{align}
L_t&=Q^{-}_{\alpha}\{\hat q^{\,\alpha/2,(-k(i))}_t-s_i\},\\
U_t&=Q^{+}_{1-\alpha}\{\hat q^{\,1-\alpha/2,(-k(i))}_t+s_i\},
\end{align}
where $Q^{-}_{\alpha}$ and $Q^{+}_{1-\alpha}$ are the $\lfloor\alpha(n{+}1)\rfloor$-th and $\lceil(1{-}\alpha)(n{+}1)\rceil$-th smallest values over the $n$ calibration snapshots, and $\hat R_t$ is the mean inner median. For exchangeable assets CV+ guarantees coverage $\ge1-2\alpha$ (typically $\approx1-\alpha$); snapshots of one asset are dependent, so the guarantee is approximate and we test it. The baseline calibrator is split CQR on three held-out bearings.

\textit{Decision and escalation.} Critical and planning horizons $\Hc$ and $\Hp$ define the actions: $r(v)=$\,\textsc{urgent} if $v\le\Hc$, \textsc{schedule} if $\Hc<v\le\Hp$, and \textsc{continue} otherwise. The Maintenance Decision Agent acts on the lower bound, $\pi_t=r(L_t)$, and turns \textsc{continue} into \textsc{inspect} when $a_t>\tau_a$. \textsc{schedule} and \textsc{urgent} are recommendations that need a person's approval and come with an explanation card (RUL, interval, anomaly evidence, top-3 SHAP~\cite{lundberg2017shap} contributors, rationale). With $B=\{\Hc,\Hp\}$, the edge escalates when its interval is ambiguous or disagrees with the anomaly evidence:
\begin{equation}
e_t=\mathbb{I}\big[\exists h{\in}B{:}\,L^e_t{\le} h{<}U^e_t\big]\lor\mathbb{I}\big[a_t{>}\tau_a\wedge r(L^e_t){=}\mathrm{C}\big],
\end{equation}
where C stands for \textsc{continue}. A local \textsc{continue} requires $L^e_t>\Hp\ge\Hc$, so a critical asset ($y_t\le\Hc$) keeps running without escalation only if $y_t\notin I^e_t$:
\begin{equation}
\Pr\big(e_t{=}0,\ \pi_t{=}\textsc{continue},\ y_t\le\Hc\big)\le\Pr(y_t\notin I^e_t).
\end{equation}
False local \textsc{urgent} actions are bounded in the same way. Unlike a width threshold, the gate ignores wide intervals that sit inside one action region and escalates narrow ones that cross a boundary.

\textit{Cost model.} We count payloads in float32. Always-cloud streams $x_t$ (112\,B per snapshot); HERA sends the 12\,B sync, plus the untransmitted part of the window when $e_t{=}1$. Latency is the edge time plus, on escalation, the RTT and the cloud time, using host timings (Sec.~\ref{sec:results}) and an assumed RTT of \rttMs\,ms.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\textit{Data.} FEMTO-ST/PRONOSTIA~\cite{nectoux2012pronostia}: \feNBearings{} bearings under three conditions, 0.1\,s snapshots every 10\,s (\feNSnap{} snapshots), full test trajectories; several fail abruptly. XJTU-SY~\cite{wang2020xjtu}: \xjNBearings{} bearings at 35\,Hz/12\,kN, 37.5\,Hz/11\,kN and 40\,Hz/10\,kN, 1.28\,s snapshots every minute (\xjNSnap{} snapshots); most degrade gradually. RUL is capped at $R_{\max}{=}\feRmax$\,s (FEMTO) and \xjRmax\,s (XJTU-SY), with $\Hc{=}0.2R_{\max}$ and $\Hp{=}0.6R_{\max}$; windows are 16/64 and 8/32 snapshots (edge/cloud). SCANIA Component X~\cite{kharazian2025scania} (DOI 10.5878/bnh5-ka77, CC BY 4.0) has \flN{} trucks with irregular operational readouts (counters and histograms); \flNf{} had a repair during the study and the rest are right-censored. We use it only for unit-level alarm timing (Sec.~\ref{sec:fleet}).

\textit{Protocol.} Bearing-grouped folds balanced by condition (\feNFolds{} for FEMTO, \xjNFolds{} for XJTU-SY) test every bearing once. Hyper-parameters (width 32--128, 8 or 25 epochs, input noise) and the edge/cloud model choice were selected once, with seed 0, on two inner validation bearings held out from the training bearings of two folds (FEMTO: \feEdgeModel/\feCloudModel+DT; XJTU-SY: \xjEdgeModel/\xjCloudModel+DT). Fold, calibration and CV+ group assignments are fixed lists shared by all three seeds, which change only the training randomness. Metrics are pooled over test bearings (mean$\pm$std). Two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests pair HERA and a comparator per bearing (seeds averaged, zero differences dropped).

\textit{Baselines and metrics.} Models are LSTM, GRU, TCN~\cite{bai2018tcn} and CNN-LSTM with and without DT context, plus MSE point models. Policies are always-edge, always-cloud and point-with-fixed-threshold; ablations drop the uncertainty (point margin), DT or anomaly term; and at HERA's budget we test anomaly-trigger (as in~\cite{saleh2026hama}), width (confidence-style deferral), linear-score and random gates. We report RMSE, MAE, coverage (Cov.), mean interval width (MPIW), the \emph{unsafe rate} (critical snapshots left at \textsc{continue}/\textsc{inspect}), false maintenance (FM: \textsc{schedule}/\textsc{urgent} while $y_t>\Hp$), escalation, payload, latency (single-thread host CPU, Apple M2 Pro; no energy) and, per bearing, the timing of the first sustained maintenance alarm.

\section{Results and Discussion}\label{sec:results}
\textit{RQ1: accuracy.} Table~\ref{tab:rul} shows two opposite regimes. On FEMTO the smallest model won: the \feEdgeParams-parameter edge model reached an RMSE of \feRmseEdge\,s with CQR-CV+, against \feRmseCloud\,s for \feCloudModel+DT. Abrupt failures leave a larger model nothing to exploit. On XJTU-SY the fog/cloud models were clearly better (best split-trained RMSE \xjBestCloudRmse\,s, \xjBestCloudModel) than the selected edge model (\xjRmseEdgeSplit\,s). DT context lowered RMSE for \feDtHelps{} of 4 architectures on FEMTO but for only \xjDtHelps{} on XJTU-SY, and cross-fitted ensembles (CV+) also improved point accuracy.

\textit{RQ2: calibration.} With only three calibration bearings, split CQR under-covered on FEMTO (\feSplitCovMin--\feSplitCovMax). CQR-CV+, which calibrates on all non-test bearings, reached \fePicpEdge{} (edge) and \fePicpCloud{} (cloud) on FEMTO and \xjPicpCloud--\xjPicpEdge{} on XJTU-SY. The price is wider intervals (MPIW \feMpiwEdge\,s and \xjMpiwCloud\,s). Coverage still varied a lot between bearings (lowest cloud coverage \feCovMinCloud{} and \xjCovMinCloud), so exchangeability between bearings remains the binding assumption. Fig.~\ref{fig:traj} shows intervals narrowing once degradation is visible, far earlier on XJTU-SY.

\textit{RQ4: unsafe decisions.} With point estimates and fixed thresholds, \feUnsafePoint\% (FEMTO) and \xjUnsafePoint\% (XJTU-SY) of critical snapshots got no maintenance action. HERA brought this down to \feUnsafeHera\% and \xjUnsafeHera\% (Table~\ref{tab:policies}). Over bearings, the differences were \feCiUnsafePt{} (FEMTO) and \xjCiUnsafePt{} (XJTU-SY) (Wilcoxon $p$\,\fePHeraPoint, $p{=}$\xjPHeraPoint), while false maintenance rose by \feCiFmPt{} and \xjCiFmPt. Sweeping $\alpha$, or a margin $\delta$ for the point policy, on the test bearings (Fig.~\ref{fig:tradeoff}a,c; descriptive, not a deployable selection) places calibrated and margin-adjusted point policies close to one frontier. Calibration thus adds a principled, held-out choice of operating point, not better attainable decisions.

\textit{RQ3 and RQ5: escalation and system trade-off.} On FEMTO the 90\% edge intervals (\feMpiwEdge\,s wide on a \feRmax\,s range) nearly always straddled $\Hp$. HERA therefore escalated \feEscHera\% of snapshots, sent more data than streaming everything to the cloud (\feBytesHera{} vs.\ \feBytesCloud\,B) and gained nothing in decisions. On XJTU-SY, HERA escalated \xjEscHera\%, matched always-cloud safety (\xjUnsafeHera\% vs.\ \xjUnsafeCloud\%) with less false maintenance (\xjFmHera\% vs.\ \xjFmCloud\%) and better RMSE (\xjRmsePolHera{} vs.\ \xjRmsePolCloud\,s), and sent \xjBytesHera{} instead of \xjBytesCloud\,B per snapshot at a mean latency of \xjLatHera{} vs.\ \xjLatCloud\,ms. The comparison with always-cloud is descriptive. The FM gap to always-cloud (\xjCiFmCl) comes from the two long-lived condition-3 bearings, and its interval reaches zero; the calibrated edge-only policy also traced a better frontier than HERA (Fig.~\ref{fig:tradeoff}c), because conservative edge intervals rarely under-predict. At a matched budget the width gate gave identical results (\xjUnsafeWidth\%/\xjFmWidth\%) and the linear-score gate nearly so (Fig.~\ref{fig:tradeoff}b,d), whereas the anomaly trigger escalated only \xjEscHeraFixed\%. Relaxing $\alpha_e$ to 0.3 cut escalation to \feEscAlphaThirty\%/\xjEscAlphaThirty\% but raised unsafe decisions to \feUnsafeAlphaThirty\%/\xjUnsafeAlphaThirty\%. Removing the DT context lowered FM on XJTU-SY (\xjFmHeraNoDT\%) at a higher unsafe rate (\xjUnsafeHeraNoDT\%), so the context does not help everywhere.

\textit{Per-bearing timing and sensitivity.} Table~\ref{tab:timing} exposes the main practical limitation. The 90\% lower bound falls below $\Hp$ early, so every calibrated policy raised its first sustained maintenance alarm while the true RUL was still above $R_{\max}$, for \fePremHera{} of \feNBearings{} FEMTO and \xjPremHera{} of \xjNBearings{} XJTU-SY bearings. The point policy was instead late for \feLatePoint{} and \xjLatePoint{} bearings. Neither is acceptable for scheduling: marginal coverage per snapshot is the wrong target for alarm timing. The safety result was stable: in all five horizon settings and all four runs with $R_{\max}$ halved or doubled (hyper-parameters not re-tuned), HERA left fewer critical snapshots without action than the point policy, and with a halved cap on XJTU-SY it reached low false maintenance at the always-cloud unsafe rate (Table~\ref{tab:timing}).

\textit{Bearing-level risk control.} We also calibrated alarm timing directly: an onset-gated two-stage alarm fires at an offset $\lambda$ chosen by conformal risk control (CRC)~\cite{angelopoulos2024crc} on the CV+ out-of-fold predictions of the non-test bearings so that $\Pr(\text{late or missing alarm})\le\varepsilon$ per bearing. At $\varepsilon{=}0.1$ the held-out late rate stayed within target (\feLateCrcTen\% FEMTO, \xjLateCrcTen\% XJTU-SY, against \feLatePointHp\% and \xjLatePointHp\% for the point policy), but only because the alarm fired early: \fePremCrcTen\% and \xjPremCrcTen\% of alarms were premature, and the onset gate escalated \feEscCrcTen\% and \xjEscCrcTen\% of snapshots. The cause is a heavy tail of required offsets (computed from true failure times): the median bearing needed an offset of \feReqOffMed{} (FEMTO) and \xjReqOffMed{} (XJTU-SY) of $R_{\max}$ to be warned in time, but the two least predictable bearings needed \feReqOffTop{} and \xjReqOffTop. This is a sample-size effect:

\textit{Proposition 1.} Let a fraction $\pi$ of $n$ calibration units be late for every admissible offset (no usable warning). CRC with the binary lateness loss returns a non-trivial offset at level $\varepsilon$ only if $(n\pi+1)/(n+1)\le\varepsilon$, i.e.\ $\pi<\varepsilon$ and $n\ge(1-\varepsilon)/(\varepsilon-\pi)$.

\textit{Proof.} The empirical risk of every offset is at least $\pi$, and CRC requires $(n\hat R+1)/(n+1)\le\varepsilon$~\cite{angelopoulos2024crc}. $\square$

For $\varepsilon{=}0.1$ and $\pi{=}0.05$ this requires $n\ge\nMinTenFive$, more than the roughly 12 bearings per fold we have. Below that, CRC has no choice but to alarm at the earliest possible offset.

\textit{Fleet-scale validation.}\label{sec:fleet} To test Proposition~1 where $n$ is large, we applied the same unit-level CRC to \flN{} Scania trucks. A logistic-regression edge model and a gradient-boosted cloud model (vehicle-grouped 5-fold cross-fitting, AUROC \flAucEdge{} and \flAucCloud) score $\Pr(\mathrm{RUL}\le\Hp)$ for each readout, with $\Hc{=}12$ and $\Hp{=}48$ time units. An alarm needs two consecutive readouts, and HERA opens a latched edge gate at half the cloud threshold. Table~\ref{tab:fleet} averages 50 random calibration/test splits. Fixed thresholds were late for \flFixEdLate\% (edge) and \flFixClLate\% (cloud) of failing trucks, while CRC hit its target (\flClBigLate\% at $\varepsilon{=}0.1$, \flHeFiveLate\% at 0.05, \flHeTwentyLate\% at 0.2). Growing the calibration fleet from 12 to 1000 failing trucks tightened the 95th-percentile late rate from \flClSmallLateQ\% to \flClBigLateQ\% and lowered false alarms from \flClSmallFa\% to \flClBigFa\%. HERA matched always-cloud decisions (\flHeBigLate\%/\flHeBigFa\% vs.\ \flClBigLate\%/\flClBigFa\%) while escalating only \flHeBigEsc\% of readouts. The edge model alone was almost as good (\flEdBigFa\% false alarms), and false alarms stayed high because the failure signal is weak.

\textit{Detection and compute.} Table~\ref{tab:detect} shows why no simple trigger is enough. The edge anomaly score reached AUROC \feDetAnomAuroc{} and \xjDetAnomAuroc{} but, at its threshold set on training data, recalled only \feDetAnomRec{} and \xjDetAnomRec. The calibrated lower bounds recalled \feDetEdgeLBRec--\xjDetEdgeLBRec{} of critical snapshots at low precision, which is the same safety/false-alarm trade-off seen in Fig.~\ref{fig:tradeoff}. Edge models need tens of thousands of MACs and under a millisecond; fog/cloud models need 20--120 times more MACs. Run unchanged in an ARM64 Linux container capped at one CPU and 1\,GB (Apple M2 Pro host, not embedded hardware), the exported models reproduced the host outputs to below \armParity, took at most \armEdgeMs\,ms (edge) and \armCloudMs\,ms (cloud) per window plus \armFeatMs\,ms for feature extraction, and peaked at \armRss\,MB of memory. Pinned to one core, p99 latency stayed below \armPinP\,ms. This only checks portability; reported latencies use host timings.

\textit{Lessons for deployment.} Table~\ref{tab:guide} condenses the evidence. (i)~\emph{Calibrate before routing}: calibrated intervals removed most unsafe point-threshold decisions on both datasets. (ii)~\emph{Hierarchy pays only if degradation shows early}: escalation helped on XJTU-SY but not on FEMTO. (iii)~\emph{Small fleets strain calibration}: three calibration bearings under-covered; cross-fitting on all assets fixed pooled coverage, yet per-asset coverage still varied. (iv)~\emph{Interval width matters more than the gate}: the decision-sufficiency and width gates behaved the same. (v)~\emph{Unit-level guarantees need fleets}: with a dozen units risk control alarms early (Proposition~1); with a thousand trucks it met its target. (vi)~\emph{Wide intervals move work to humans}: approval load, not model accuracy, limits human-centric use. Limitations: 32 bearings, approximate exchangeability, a state-based DT, no embedded-hardware timing, analytic communication, and no deployment or user study.

\section{Conclusion}
Cross-fitted conformal RUL intervals removed most of the unsafe decisions that point-threshold maintenance makes on two bearing datasets. Using the same intervals to gate edge-to-cloud escalation paid off on gradually degrading bearings and on a truck fleet, where HERA matched always-cloud safety with a fraction of the cloud traffic. It did not pay off under abrupt failures, and our decision-sufficiency rule was no better than a width gate. Calibrating alarm timing with conformal risk control works only with enough units (Proposition~1): with a dozen bearings it alarmed early, while on \flN{} trucks it met its lateness target, at a false-alarm cost set by the weak failure signal. We therefore see better early-warning features, not smarter routing rules, as the next lever.
