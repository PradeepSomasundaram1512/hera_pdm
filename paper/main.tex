\begin{abstract}
Edge--cloud predictive maintenance (PdM) must decide when an edge prediction is good enough to act on locally. We use HERA-PdM, in which conformalized quantile regression with CV+ (CQR-CV+) gives remaining-useful-life (RUL) intervals and the edge escalates only when its interval spans a maintenance-decision boundary, to ask when such uncertainty gating is worth it. On 17 FEMTO and 15 XJTU-SY run-to-failure bearings, calibrated decisions left fewer critical snapshots without action than point thresholds (\feUnsafeHera\% vs.\ \feUnsafePoint\% and \xjUnsafeHera\% vs.\ \xjUnsafePoint\%) but raised false maintenance sharply. Pooled coverage reached \xjPicpCloud--\xjPicpEdge, yet \xjPbCovLow{} of 15 XJTU-SY bearings fell below the nominal 0.90. Escalation helped only under gradual degradation (XJTU-SY); under abrupt failures (FEMTO) the edge escalated \feEscHera\% of snapshots. A simple interval-width gate gave the same unsafe rate on every bearing and near-identical false maintenance, so uncertainty quality mattered more than routing sophistication. Unit-level risk control of alarm timing was infeasible with about 12 bearings per fold, as a finite-sample condition predicts, but met its target on \flN{} Scania trucks, where a calibrated edge-only model nearly matched the hierarchy. Wide intervals shifted work to human approvers.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Predictive maintenance (PdM) estimates the remaining useful life (RUL) of a machine so that repairs can be planned before failure~\cite{lei2018machinery}. In practice it is judged by the decisions it triggers: missed failures, unnecessary interventions, and work for the people who approve actions, whom Industry~5.0 keeps in charge~\cite{xu2021industry5,vanoudenhoven2023pdm5}.

Edge--cloud architectures keep routine inference near the machine and reserve larger models for difficult cases~\cite{ren2021cloudedge}, so they need a rule for when local evidence suffices: escalating everything wastes bandwidth, escalating too little risks unsafe decisions.

Existing hierarchical PdM systems escalate on anomaly thresholds~\cite{saleh2026hama} or on uncalibrated model confidence~\cite{yao2026ecdistill}. Work on selective prediction and cascades offers principled deferral~\cite{geifman2017selective,huang2025cab} and shows that simple confidence-based deferral often suffices but can fail~\cite{jitkrittum2023cascade}.

Conformal prediction gives distribution-free intervals~\cite{romano2019cqr,barber2021jackknife}, including for RUL~\cite{javanmardi2023conformal,wang2026conformal}, and is a natural basis for such a rule. Whether it helps in PdM depends on conditions benchmarks rarely vary: whether degradation is observable early enough for the edge interval to separate actions; whether there are enough exchangeable assets to calibrate on, since guarantees are marginal over units~\cite{foygel2021limits,dunn2023hierarchical}; and how the decision structure converts interval width into escalations and approvals.

This paper studies those conditions, using HERA-PdM as an experimental framework rather than a claimed best system, on two bearing datasets with contrasting degradation and a truck fleet.

Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item A calibrated edge--cloud PdM evaluation framework in which conformal RUL intervals drive a decision-aware escalation rule, compared against point, width, score, anomaly and random gates at matched budgets.
\item Cross-dataset evidence that hierarchy pays off under gradual degradation but not under abrupt failures or wide edge intervals, with bearing-level statistics and seed and architecture robustness checks.
\item A finite-sample feasibility condition for unit-level conformal risk control, contrasted empirically between bearing cohorts and a fleet of \flN{} trucks.
\item An analysis of safety, false maintenance, communication, latency and human approval load, including negative results against simpler baselines.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}
\textit{Prognostics and uncertainty.} Reviews~\cite{lei2018machinery} treat detecting when degradation becomes observable as a prerequisite for prognosis. Uncertainty for deep prognostics has been benchmarked~\cite{basora2025benchmark}, and conformal RUL intervals exist~\cite{javanmardi2023conformal,wang2026conformal}, but without a routing or decision layer.
\textit{Hierarchical PdM.} Ren et~al.~\cite{ren2021cloudedge} refine edge RUL estimates in the cloud; HAMA~\cite{saleh2026hama} escalates from an edge z-score filter to fog ensembles; EC-Distill-ZeroDiag~\cite{yao2026ecdistill} offloads uncertain diagnosis cases from an edge agent.
\textit{Deferral and conformal guarantees.} Selective classification abstains to meet a risk target~\cite{geifman2017selective}; conformal alignment certifies edge--cloud cascades for classification~\cite{huang2025cab}; and cascade theory characterizes when confidence-based deferral suffices~\cite{jitkrittum2023cascade}. Conformal risk control~\cite{angelopoulos2024crc} bounds monotone losses. Coverage is marginal, and distribution-free conditional coverage is impossible in general~\cite{foygel2021limits}; hierarchical and non-exchangeable settings need adapted procedures~\cite{dunn2023hierarchical,barber2023beyond}.

\textit{Positioning.} A structured search of Crossref, Semantic Scholar and arXiv (2020--2026, 23 query strings; 42 papers coded on 11 dimensions, coding matrix available from the author) found no study that uses conformal RUL intervals computed on the edge as the escalation rule of a hierarchical PdM system and tests it against simpler gates across degradation regimes (Table~\ref{tab:related}). The architecture is not new; the contribution is the evaluation.

\section{Evaluation Framework}
\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/fig1_architecture.pdf}
\caption{HERA-PdM. The edge computes a calibrated RUL interval $I^e_t$ and escalates only if $I^e_t$ does not determine a unique action; the fog/cloud model uses the state context $c_t$; schedule/urgent actions require human approval.}
\label{fig:arch}
\end{figure}

Fig.~\ref{fig:arch} shows the four layers. Its ``agents'' are software modules with fixed responsibilities and messages; no language model is used and no claim rests on this abstraction.

\textit{Edge monitoring.} Each vibration snapshot is reduced to 28 features $x_t$ (per axis: RMS, kurtosis, skewness, peak-to-peak, crest, impulse and shape factors, spectral centroid and six log band energies), z-scored against the asset's first snapshots and compressed by $\mathrm{sign}(z)\log(1{+}|z|)$, giving the health index $h_t=\log(1+\frac1d\sum_j|z_{t,j}|)$. The anomaly score $a_t$, the smoothed $h_t$ standardized by the asset's raw baseline, alarms above $\tau_a$, the 99th percentile of healthy training snapshots. A small model maps a short window to three RUL quantiles.

\textit{State-based Digital Twin context.} Our ``Digital Twin'' (DT) is a lightweight state-based digital representation, not a full twin with seamless cyber-physical integration as surveyed in~\cite{tao2019dt}. It stores condition, sensor state, $h_t$, a context vector $c_t\in\mathbb{R}^6$, the latest RUL estimate, interval and action, and is updated by a 12-byte sync $(t,h_t,a_t)$ per snapshot. $c_t$ holds fast and slow averages of $h_t$, their difference, its running maximum, its slope and the cumulative excess over baseline; absolute operating time is excluded because lifetimes differ by an order of magnitude.

\textit{Prognostics.} A sequence model takes a longer window $X_t$ and $c_t$ and outputs non-crossing quantiles $(\hat q^{\,\alpha/2}_t,\hat q^{\,0.5}_t,\hat q^{\,1-\alpha/2}_t)$, trained with the pinball loss on $y_t=\min(\mathrm{RUL}_t,R_{\max})$.

\textit{Calibration (CQR-CV+).} The non-test assets are split into $K{=}4$ condition-balanced groups; an inner model $g^{(-k)}$ is trained without group $k$, giving out-of-fold scores $s_i=\max(\hat q^{\,\alpha/2}_i-y_i,\,y_i-\hat q^{\,1-\alpha/2}_i)$. Following CV+~\cite{barber2021jackknife} with CQR scores~\cite{romano2019cqr},
\begin{align}
L_t&=Q^{-}_{\alpha}\{\hat q^{\,\alpha/2,(-k(i))}_t-s_i\},\\
U_t&=Q^{+}_{1-\alpha}\{\hat q^{\,1-\alpha/2,(-k(i))}_t+s_i\},
\end{align}
where $Q^{-}_{\alpha}$ and $Q^{+}_{1-\alpha}$ are the $\lfloor\alpha(n{+}1)\rfloor$-th and $\lceil(1{-}\alpha)(n{+}1)\rceil$-th smallest values over the $n$ calibration snapshots. Under exchangeability, CV+ guarantees marginal coverage $\ge1-2\alpha$ (typically $\approx1-\alpha$). Snapshots within an asset are temporally dependent, so this holds at best at the level of exchangeable assets~\cite{dunn2023hierarchical}; our snapshot-level evaluation is approximate, and no per-asset (conditional) guarantee is implied~\cite{foygel2021limits}. Split CQR on three held-out bearings is the baseline calibrator.

\textit{Decision and escalation.} With critical and planning horizons $\Hc<\Hp$, $r(v)=$\,\textsc{urgent} if $v\le\Hc$, \textsc{schedule} if $\Hc<v\le\Hp$, else \textsc{continue}. Actions follow the lower bound, $\pi_t=r(L_t)$, with \textsc{continue} raised to \textsc{inspect} when $a_t>\tau_a$. \textsc{schedule} and \textsc{urgent} are recommendations that the implemented logic routes to a person for approval, with RUL, interval, anomaly evidence and top-3 SHAP~\cite{lundberg2017shap} attributions as context; SHAP here supplies feature attributions, not validated explanations. With $B=\{\Hc,\Hp\}$ the edge escalates when its interval is ambiguous or contradicts the anomaly evidence:
\begin{equation}
e_t=\mathbb{I}\big[\exists h{\in}B{:}\,L^e_t{\le} h{<}U^e_t\big]\lor\mathbb{I}\big[a_t{>}\tau_a\wedge r(L^e_t){=}\mathrm{C}\big],
\end{equation}
where C stands for \textsc{continue}. A local \textsc{continue} requires $L^e_t>\Hp\ge\Hc$, so a critical asset ($y_t\le\Hc$) keeps running without escalation only if $y_t\notin I^e_t$:
\begin{equation}
\Pr\big(e_t{=}0,\ \pi_t{=}\textsc{continue},\ y_t\le\Hc\big)\le\Pr(y_t\notin I^e_t).
\end{equation}
The bound is only as good as the (approximate) edge coverage. Unlike a width threshold, the gate ignores wide intervals inside one action region and escalates narrow ones that cross a boundary; we test whether this matters.

\textit{Cost model.} Payloads are counted in float32: always-cloud streams $x_t$ (112\,B per snapshot); HERA sends the 12\,B sync plus the untransmitted part of the window when $e_t{=}1$. Latency is the edge time plus, on escalation, the RTT and cloud time, using host timings (Sec.~\ref{sec:results}) and an assumed RTT of \rttMs\,ms.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\textit{Data.} FEMTO-ST/PRONOSTIA~\cite{nectoux2012pronostia}: \feNBearings{} bearings under three conditions, 0.1\,s snapshots every 10\,s (\feNSnap{} snapshots), full test trajectories; several fail abruptly. XJTU-SY~\cite{wang2020xjtu}: \xjNBearings{} bearings at 35\,Hz/12\,kN, 37.5\,Hz/11\,kN and 40\,Hz/10\,kN, 1.28\,s snapshots every minute (\xjNSnap{} snapshots); most degrade gradually. RUL is capped at $R_{\max}{=}\feRmax$\,s (FEMTO) and \xjRmax\,s (XJTU-SY), with $\Hc{=}0.2R_{\max}$ and $\Hp{=}0.6R_{\max}$; windows are 16/64 and 8/32 snapshots (edge/cloud). SCANIA Component X~\cite{kharazian2025scania} (DOI 10.5878/bnh5-ka77, CC BY 4.0) has \flN{} trucks with irregular operational readouts; \flNf{} had a repair during the study and the rest are right-censored.

\textit{Protocol.} Condition-balanced, bearing-grouped folds (\feNFolds{} for FEMTO, \xjNFolds{} for XJTU-SY) test every bearing once. Per-architecture hyper-parameters (width 32--128, 8 or 25 epochs, input noise) and the edge/cloud architectures were chosen once, with seed 0, by pinball loss on two inner validation bearings held out from the training bearings of two folds (FEMTO: \feEdgeModel/\feCloudModel+DT; XJTU-SY: \xjEdgeModel/\xjCloudModel+DT), then frozen for all folds and seeds; test bearings were never used for selection. The protocol was revised once, after a first run showed overfitting and under-coverage, by adding this tuning, CV+ and XJTU-SY; the changes are documented with the code. Fold, calibration and CV+ assignments are fixed lists shared by all seeds, which change only the training randomness. Three seeds capture limited training variability; we therefore repeat the key comparisons with five seeds and with the runner-up architectures.

\textit{Baselines and metrics.} Models: LSTM, GRU, TCN~\cite{bai2018tcn} and CNN-LSTM with and without DT context, plus MSE point models. Policies: always-edge and always-cloud (both calibrated), point-with-fixed-threshold, and HERA with ablations. At HERA's escalation budget, matched on calibration data, we compare anomaly-trigger (as in~\cite{saleh2026hama}), width (confidence-style deferral), linear-score and random gates. We report RMSE, coverage, interval width (MPIW), the \emph{unsafe rate} (critical snapshots left at \textsc{continue}/\textsc{inspect}), false maintenance (FM: \textsc{schedule}/\textsc{urgent} while $y_t>\Hp$), escalation, payload, latency (single-thread host CPU, Apple M2 Pro; no energy) and the timing of each bearing's first sustained alarm.

\textit{Statistical units and inference.} For the bearing datasets the unit is the bearing. For each bearing we compute a policy's unsafe or FM rate over its snapshots, average it over seeds, and compare HERA with a comparator by a two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test over bearings~\cite{demsar2006statistical}, dropping zero differences and reporting the number of non-zero pairs $n_{\neq}$; with $n_{\neq}<5$ no test is run. Holm correction over the five primary tests (HERA vs.\ point, unsafe and FM, both datasets; vs.\ edge-only FM, XJTU-SY) gives a largest adjusted $p$ of \holmMaxP. Bearings without critical (or healthy) snapshots are excluded from that metric. Bootstrap 95\% CIs resample bearings. Snapshots, seeds and inner models are never treated as independent units. Trade-off curves, sensitivity runs and comparisons with always-cloud are descriptive. On SCANIA the unit is the truck, and results are means over 50 random calibration/test splits of trucks.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}
\textit{Accuracy and calibration.} Table~\ref{tab:rul} shows opposite regimes. On FEMTO the small edge model had the lowest RMSE (\feRmseEdge\,s vs.\ \feRmseCloud\,s for \feCloudModel+DT, CQR-CV+), as abrupt failures leave a larger model little to exploit. On XJTU-SY the fog/cloud models had lower RMSE (best split-trained \xjBestCloudRmse\,s, \xjBestCloudModel) than the edge model (\xjRmseEdgeSplit\,s). DT context lowered RMSE for \feDtHelps{} of 4 architectures on FEMTO but \xjDtHelps{} on XJTU-SY. Split CQR with three calibration bearings under-covered on FEMTO (\feSplitCovMin--\feSplitCovMax); CQR-CV+ reached pooled coverage of \fePicpEdge{}/\fePicpCloud{} (edge/cloud) on FEMTO and \xjPicpEdge{}/\xjPicpCloud{} on XJTU-SY, with wider intervals. Pooled coverage hides large differences between assets: the median bearing's cloud coverage was \fePbCovMed{} (FEMTO) and \xjPbCovMed{} (XJTU-SY), \fePbCovLow{} of 17 and \xjPbCovLow{} of 15 bearings fell below 0.90, and the worst were \fePbCovMin{} and \xjPbCovMin. Good marginal coverage did not mean reliable intervals for every asset.

\textit{Calibration improves safety, not false maintenance.} Point estimates with fixed thresholds left \feUnsafePoint\% (FEMTO) and \xjUnsafePoint\% (XJTU-SY) of critical snapshots without action, against \feUnsafeHera\% and \xjUnsafeHera\% for HERA (Table~\ref{tab:policies}); per bearing, the differences were \feCiUnsafePt{} ($n_{\neq}{=}\feNzHeraPoint$, $p$\,\fePHeraPoint) and \xjCiUnsafePt{} ($n_{\neq}{=}\xjNzHeraPoint$, $p{=}$\xjPHeraPoint). False maintenance rose by \feCiFmPt{} and \xjCiFmPt{} ($n_{\neq}{=}\feNzFmHeraPoint$ and \xjNzFmHeraPoint, both $p$\,\fePFmHeraPoint). Sweeping $\alpha$, or a margin $\delta$ for the point policy (Fig.~\ref{fig:tradeoff}a,c), places both families close to one frontier, so calibration contributes a principled choice of operating point from held-out data rather than decisions no tuned margin could reach.

\textit{When does hierarchy help?} Under abrupt failures it did not. On FEMTO the 90\% edge intervals (\feMpiwEdge\,s on a \feRmax\,s range) almost always straddled $\Hp$, so HERA escalated \feEscHera\% of snapshots, sent more data than always-cloud streaming (\feBytesHera{} vs.\ \feBytesCloud\,B) and matched the calibrated edge-only policy on every bearing's unsafe rate. Under gradual degradation it helped. On XJTU-SY, HERA escalated \xjEscHera\% and had lower FM than calibrated edge-only inference on \xjNzFmHeraEdge{} of 13 bearings ($p$\,\xjPFmHeraEdge; \xjFmHera\% vs.\ \xjFmEdge\%), at the price of a higher unsafe rate on \xjNzHeraEdge{} bearings (\xjUnsafeHera\% vs.\ \xjUnsafeEdge\%). Relative to always-cloud it was descriptively similar: same unsafe rate (\xjUnsafeHera\%), lower FM (\xjFmHera\% vs.\ \xjFmCloud\%, CI \xjCiFmCl, only \xjNzFmHeraCloud{} bearings differing) and \xjBytesHera{} instead of \xjBytesCloud\,B per snapshot.

\textit{Routing sophistication mattered less than uncertainty quality.} At a matched budget, the width gate gave the same unsafe rate as HERA's decision-aware gate on all 17 FEMTO and 15 XJTU-SY bearings; FM differed on only \feRobNzFmWidth{} and \xjRobNzFmWidth{} bearings, by \feRobFmWidthDiff{} and \xjRobFmWidthDiff{} percentage points on average. The linear-score gate was nearly identical (Fig.~\ref{fig:tradeoff}b,d); the anomaly trigger escalated only \xjEscHeraFixed\% on XJTU-SY and inherited edge-only behaviour, and the random gate, at the same budget, gave higher FM (\xjFmRandom\% vs.\ \xjFmHera\%). Relaxing $\alpha_e$ to 0.3 cut escalation to \feEscAlphaThirty\%/\xjEscAlphaThirty\% but raised unsafe decisions to \feUnsafeAlphaThirty\%/\xjUnsafeAlphaThirty\%.

\textit{Robustness.} With five seeds, HERA left \feRobFiveUnsafeHera\% and \xjRobFiveUnsafeHera\% of critical snapshots without action, against \feRobFiveUnsafePoint\% and \xjRobFiveUnsafePoint\% for point thresholds, and the width gate again matched its unsafe rate on every bearing. With the runner-up architectures (\feRobAltEdge/\feRobAltCloud+DT and \xjRobAltEdge/\xjRobAltCloud+DT), the safety gain held (\feRobAltUnsafeHera\% vs.\ \feRobAltUnsafePoint\% and \xjRobAltUnsafeHera\% vs.\ \xjRobAltUnsafePoint\%), but wider edge intervals raised escalation to \feRobAltEscHera\% and \xjRobAltEscHera\%. On FEMTO, HERA's FM was then lower than the width gate's on \feRobAltNzFmWidth{} of 17 bearings ($p$ \feRobAltPFmWidth), by only \feRobAltFmWidthDiff{} percentage points. The conclusions hold, but the hierarchy's value depends on edge interval width.

\textit{Per-snapshot coverage does not give timely alarms.} Because the 90\% lower bound falls below $\Hp$ early, every calibrated policy raised its first sustained alarm while the true RUL still exceeded $R_{\max}$ for \fePremHera{} of 17 FEMTO and \xjPremHera{} of 15 XJTU-SY bearings, whereas the point policy was late for \feLatePoint{} and \xjLatePoint{} bearings (Table~\ref{tab:timing}). Neither suits scheduling. The safety ordering was descriptively stable: in five horizon settings and four runs with $R_{\max}$ halved or doubled (hyper-parameters not re-tuned), HERA left fewer critical snapshots without action than the point policy.

\textit{Unit-level risk control needs many units.} We then calibrated alarm timing directly: an onset-gated alarm fires at an offset $\lambda$ chosen by conformal risk control (CRC)~\cite{angelopoulos2024crc} on the CV+ out-of-fold predictions of non-test bearings so that $\Pr(\text{late or missing alarm})\le\varepsilon$, with bearings as the exchangeable units. At $\varepsilon{=}0.1$ the held-out late rate met the target (\feLateCrcTen\% and \xjLateCrcTen\%, against \feLatePointHp\% and \xjLatePointHp\% for the point policy) only by alarming early: \fePremCrcTen\% and \xjPremCrcTen\% of alarms were premature. The median bearing needed an offset of \feReqOffMed{} and \xjReqOffMed{} of $R_{\max}$ to be warned in time, the two least predictable bearings \feReqOffTop{} and \xjReqOffTop{} (computed from true failure times). A simple finite-sample condition explains this:

\textit{Proposition 1 (feasibility).} If a fraction $\pi$ of $n$ calibration units is late for every admissible offset, CRC with the binary lateness loss returns a non-trivial offset at level $\varepsilon$ only if $(n\pi+1)/(n+1)\le\varepsilon$, i.e.\ $\pi<\varepsilon$ and $n\ge(1-\varepsilon)/(\varepsilon-\pi)$.

\textit{Proof.} Every offset has empirical risk at least $\pi$, and CRC requires $(n\hat R+1)/(n+1)\le\varepsilon$~\cite{angelopoulos2024crc}. $\square$ For $\varepsilon{=}0.1$ and $\pi{=}0.05$ this requires $n\ge\nMinTenFive$, more than the roughly 12 bearings per fold available; below it, CRC must alarm at the earliest offset.

\textit{Fleet-scale test of the sample-size argument.}\label{sec:fleet} The SCANIA study tests only this unit-level risk-control argument, not the bearing architecture: logistic-regression (edge) and gradient-boosted (cloud) classifiers, cross-fitted over vehicle-grouped folds (AUROC \flAucEdge{} and \flAucCloud), score $\Pr(\mathrm{RUL}\le\Hp)$ per readout with $\Hc{=}12$ and $\Hp{=}48$ time units, without RUL intervals, DT context or SHAP. ``HERA'' here is a latched edge gate that escalates to the cloud score at half the cloud threshold; alarms need two consecutive readouts. Fixed thresholds were late for \flFixEdLate\% (edge) and \flFixClLate\% (cloud) of failing trucks; CRC met its target (Table~\ref{tab:fleet}: \flClBigLate\% at $\varepsilon{=}0.1$, \flHeFiveLate\% at 0.05, \flHeTwentyLate\% at 0.2). Growing the calibration set from 12 to 1000 failing trucks tightened the 95th-percentile late rate from \flClSmallLateQ\% to \flClBigLateQ\% and lowered false alarms from \flClSmallFa\% to \flClBigFa\%, as the sample-size argument predicts. Calibrated edge-only alarms were nearly as good as the hierarchy (\flEdBigLate\%/\flEdBigFa\% vs.\ \flHeBigLate\%/\flHeBigFa\% late/false alarms) without sending any readouts, whereas HERA sent \flHeBigEsc\%. False alarms stayed high, reflecting a weak failure signal.

\textit{Detection, compute and approval load.} No simple trigger sufficed: the anomaly score reached AUROC \feDetAnomAuroc{} and \xjDetAnomAuroc{} but, at its training threshold, recall of only \feDetAnomRec{} and \xjDetAnomRec, while calibrated lower bounds recalled \feDetEdgeLBRec--\xjDetEdgeLBRec{} of critical snapshots at low precision. Edge models need under a millisecond on the host and 20--120 times fewer MACs than fog/cloud models. The implemented approval logic sent \feApprHera\% (FEMTO) and \xjApprHera\% (XJTU-SY) of HERA snapshots to a person; the usefulness of the SHAP context (additivity error \feShapRel\%/\xjShapRel\%) to operators was not tested.

\section{Discussion}
\textit{Lesson 1: calibration improves safety but not false maintenance.} It moved the operating point along an almost unchanged frontier.

\textit{Lesson 2: hierarchy helps only when degradation is observable early}, so that edge intervals separate action regions (XJTU-SY); otherwise it costs more bandwidth than streaming (FEMTO).

\textit{Lesson 3: gate sophistication matters less than uncertainty quality.} The decision-aware gate and a width gate were practically indistinguishable, consistent with cascade theory showing that simple confidence-based deferral often suffices~\cite{jitkrittum2023cascade}. Effort is better spent on narrower, better-calibrated edge intervals than on routing logic.

\textit{Lesson 4: unit-level guarantees need enough independent assets.} Pooled coverage hid badly under-covered bearings, and risk control needed a fleet, not a dozen bearings (Proposition~1).

\textit{Lesson 5: wide intervals shift burden to people} (\feApprHera\% and \xjApprHera\% of snapshots needed approval), and decision-makers often do not adopt system-generated PdM advice~\cite{vanoudenhoven2023pdm5}.

\textit{When is a hierarchy justified over calibrated edge-only inference?} In our data, only when the cloud model is more accurate and degradation shows early enough for that to change actions (XJTU-SY). With equally accurate tiers (SCANIA) or abrupt failures (FEMTO), calibrated edge-only inference gave nearly the same decisions without transmission. Bandwidth, cloud-model advantage, interval width and operator capacity should decide.

\textit{Limitations.} 32 bearings in total; three training seeds, complemented by one five-seed check; approximate exchangeability and heterogeneous per-bearing coverage; a lightweight state-based DT; analytic communication; no timing on embedded hardware, no physical deployment and no operator study, so explanation usefulness and approval effects are unvalidated; and SCANIA tests the risk-control scaling, not the full bearing pipeline.

\section{Conclusion}
Calibrated uncertainty reduced unsafe maintenance decisions relative to point thresholds, but uncertainty-gated hierarchy was justified only when the edge interval separated maintenance actions, as under gradual but not abrupt degradation. Across bearings and a truck fleet, uncertainty quality and the number of calibration units mattered more than routing sophistication: a width gate matched our decision-aware gate, calibrated edge-only inference nearly matched the hierarchy on the fleet, and unit-level risk control needed hundreds of units, not a dozen. Earlier-warning features and group-aware calibration are the next levers, not more elaborate routing.
